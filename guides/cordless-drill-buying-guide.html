How to Choose a Cordless Drill Without Overpaying

A good drill is one that fits the work you actually do. You don't always need the biggest battery, highest voltage, or most expensive kit. Use these eight checks before buying.

Quick answer: For furniture assembly and occasional household repairs, prioritize a lightweight drill/driver with a comfortable grip and a clutch. For frequent drilling in wood or metal, consider a brushless 18V/20V-class model with two batteries. For masonry, check whether you need a hammer-drill function and the correct bits.

1. Drill/driver vs. impact driver vs. hammer drill

A drill/driver is the most versatile first purchase: it drills holes and drives screws with an adjustable clutch. An impact driver excels at driving long screws and bolts but isn't a direct replacement for a drill. A hammer drill adds a percussive action useful for some brick and masonry tasks; hard concrete may require a rotary hammer.

2. Voltage is only part of the story

Compact 12V-class tools can be excellent for light jobs and tight spaces. The 18V/20V-class is a popular all-around category. Note that some brands market the maximum charged voltage as 20V while others describe the same general battery class as 18V nominal. Compare actual performance, size, and the batteries included—not the number on the box alone.

3. Brushless vs. brushed motors

Brushless drills can offer better efficiency, less maintenance, and strong performance, often at a higher price. A brushed drill can still be a good value for occasional use. Don't pay a premium for brushless if the less expensive tool comfortably meets your needs.

4. Batteries: count and capacity matter

Battery capacity is measured in amp-hours (Ah). Higher Ah generally means more runtime within the same battery platform, but also more weight. Two modest batteries can be more convenient than one large battery because one can charge while you work. Check whether the listing includes batteries and a charger—some listings are tool-only.

5. Torque, speed, and clutch settings

Torque helps turn larger bits and fasteners, while variable speed gives you control. Multiple clutch settings reduce the chance of stripping screws. Don't compare advertised torque figures across brands as if they were measured under identical conditions; test methods can vary.

6. Chuck size and compatibility

A 3/8-inch chuck suits many basic tasks. A 1/2-inch chuck accepts larger-shank bits and is common on heavier-duty drills. Confirm the drill accepts the bits you plan to use.

7. Weight, grip, and work light

If you're assembling cabinets or working overhead, a lighter, shorter drill may be more useful than a more powerful model. Consider grip comfort, balance with the battery installed, and whether the work light illuminates the actual drilling point.

Which drill should you buy?

Occasional DIY: A compact drill/driver with a clutch and one or two batteries. Regular home projects: An 18V/20V-class brushless drill kit with two batteries. Deck building and heavy fastening: Consider a drill/driver plus an impact driver. Masonry: Choose the appropriate hammer or rotary-hammer tool for the material.

Before purchasing, verify the exact model number, included accessories, seller reputation, shipping cost, and return policy. Prices and inventory change.
